### Hydraulic Head

We refreshed our memories that _hydraulic head_ was a key metric for understanding groundwater conditions, and recalled that, despite typically being measured in units of _length,_ this is actually a measure of _energy._ To understand why, think back to physics, where "boxes" were evaluated for potential energy or momentum. A similar thing can be done for packets of water, but since the density is typically constant, the energy terms simplify to length. This is generally what was done when we studied Bernoulli's equation in fluid mechanics (try reviewing [this part of the Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli%27s_principle#Incompressible_flow_equation) to see the complete equation). Note that for flow in porous media (i.e., most groundwater flow), the velocity term typically drops off (to be discussed later). This results in the common way of evaluating hydraulic head as the simple sum of elevation and pressure head (we will practice more with this next week). Try simplifying the equation as an exercise.

### Darcy's Law, Energy and Conservation

Elsewhere in the discussion we hinted at Darcy's Law (more next week!) and discussed how much of the course can be thought of as solving Darcy's Law and a Conservation Equation. A good example of conservation of mass came from something you may remember from other courses, where you looked at a flow rate of some "stuff" into and out of a volume of interest (i.e., $Q_{in}$ and $Q_{out}$). If they are not equal, the amount of "stuff" will either increase or decrease. This can be generalized as:

$$
I-O=\Delta S
$$

or, _input minus output equals change in storage._ There is a [simple diagram available for reference in the online Engineering Toolbox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/conservation-mass-d_182.html#gsc.tab=0). This concept covers any quantity being conserved (i.e., mass, contaminant concentration, etc.). For hydraulic problems, it turns out mass and energy are useful quantities to conserve. We will see this in action later on in the course. For now, note that when the in and out are equal, we refer to a _Steady-State_ problem. This means that, _from the control volume perspective,_ there is not change in the system state with time. These are the most simple of groundwater problems to solve, and we will return to steady-state problems once we cover a few key properties of porous media.
